In my [last post on the Kenopanishad]({% post_url general/2026-11-01-kenopanishad-beyond-the-known-and-the-unknown %}), I mused about how the intellect is a tool in the hands of the knower and cannot turn around and grasp the knower itself. I want to stay with the intellect a little longer, because there is another habit of the intellect that the Upanishads seem to have spotted thousands of years before we built our laboratories. The intellect loves to take things apart. <!--more-->

## The intellect's favourite trick

Give the intellect anything at all and its first instinct is to break it down into its constituent parts. Understand the parts, it says, add them up, and you will understand the whole. A car is an engine, a chassis and four wheels. A body is organs, organs are cells, cells are molecules. This method has given us medicine, engineering and the device that you are reading this on, so I am the last person to sneer at it. I have spent a whole career decomposing large systems into smaller ones!

But watch what happens when the intellect turns this trick on itself. It explains thought by breaking the brain down into the collective work of some eighty-odd billion neurons and the trillions of connections between them. It is a remarkable picture. And yet, somewhere in that picture, the one who is doing the explaining quietly goes missing. Wordsworth saw this coming more than two centuries ago:

> Our meddling intellect  
> Mis-shapes the beauteous forms of things:—  
> We murder to dissect.

## A chastened son and a banyan seed

The Chandogya Upanishad has a wonderful story about this. Swetaketu, the son of the great rishi Uddalaka Aruni, goes away at twelve to study the Vedas and comes back at twenty-four, very pleased with himself. The Upanishad does not mince words. It calls him conceited, proud of his learning and arrogant. His father lets him have his moment and then asks him a quiet question. "Did you ask for that teaching by which the unheard becomes heard, the unthought becomes thought and the unknown becomes known?" Swetaketu has no idea what his father is talking about. All those years of learning and nobody taught him *that*. (Those of us who have walked out of universities thinking we had the world figured out will find this a little too close to home.)

What follows is one of the most beautiful teaching sequences in all of the Upanishads. In the twelfth section of the sixth chapter, Uddalaka asks the now chastened Swetaketu to fetch a fruit from the banyan tree. Here it is in my own rendering:

> "Bring me a fruit of the banyan tree."
>
> "Here it is, sir."
>
> "Break it."
>
> "It is broken, sir."
>
> "What do you see in it?"
>
> "These tiny seeds, sir."
>
> "Break one of them."
>
> "It is broken, sir."
>
> "What do you see in it?"
>
> "Nothing at all, sir."

Look at what Uddalaka has made his son do. He has made him perform the intellect's favourite experiment. Break the whole into parts, then break the part into smaller parts. And at the end of the dissection, the honest answer is *na kiñcana* - nothing at all. Then comes the line that I find so moving:

> तं होवाच यं वै सोम्यैतमणिमानं न निभालयस एतस्य वै सोम्यैषोऽणिम्न एवं महान्न्यग्रोधस्तिष्ठति श्रद्धत्स्व सोम्येति ॥
>
> *taṃ hovāca yaṃ vai somyaitam aṇimānaṃ na nibhālayasa etasya vai somyaiṣo'ṇimna evaṃ mahān nyagrodhas tiṣṭhati śraddhatsva somyeti*

"My dear, that subtle essence which you cannot see - it is from that very essence that this great banyan tree stands. Have faith, my dear."

## Where does the tree come from?

There are a few ways of thinking about how an enormous banyan tree comes out of a seed in which you can see nothing at all.

The first is that the seed already contains the entire tree. It is all there in the DNA, and the soil, the water and the sun only help it unfold. This is close to what science believes today. Even here, biologists will tell you that the genome is more of a recipe than a blueprint. The same seed in different soil gives you a rather different tree. But broadly, the tree is in the seed.

The second is that the tree is manufactured by an external agent who uses the seed as a rough blueprint but brings a considerable amount of creativity of his own. In this view the seed is foundational, but it does not contain all the intelligence that is needed to produce the tree. The potter needs the clay, but the pot is not in the clay.

There is also something in between, advocated by some, of an organism that is self-evolving and somehow pulls itself up by its own bootstraps. I suspect that even its staunchest votaries only half believe in it 🙃.

## Uddalaka's answer

Uddalaka does not pick any of these. He says that it is all about Brahman, the invisible being that causes these things to happen and that also *becomes* these things. Interestingly, this is neither of the first two options and both of them at the same time. Vedanta calls Brahman *abhinna-nimitta-upādāna-kāraṇa* - the cause that is at once the material and the maker. It is the clay and the potter together. (The Brahma Sutras argue that it has to be so, precisely because of Uddalaka's opening promise to his son that by knowing one thing, everything else becomes known.) The Mundaka Upanishad gives a lovely image for this - just as a spider spins its web out of itself and draws it back in, the universe comes forth from the Imperishable.

A few things stand out for me from this little passage.

**Subtle cause, vast effect.** Just as a huge tree lies latent in a microscopic seed, the vast manifest universe comes from a subtle, non-dual reality. There is a saying, often attributed to Robert Schuller, that anyone can count the seeds in an apple but only God can count the apples in a seed. The intellect is very good at the first kind of counting. It is quite helpless with the second.

**Invisibility is not non-existence.** What the eye cannot see is not empty nothingness. Swetaketu said "nothing at all" and he was being perfectly honest. Uddalaka does not contradict him. He simply tells him that the nothing he is looking at is the essence from which everything stands. It is the same point that the Kenopanishad made - unknowable to the intellect does not mean unreal. The map, as Korzybski reminded us, is not the territory. Just because a thing does not show up on our map does not mean that it is not there.

**The whole is not the sum of its parts.** The intellect assumes that if you account for all the parts, you have accounted for the whole. The banyan seed says otherwise. Sum up all the parts of the seed and you get nothing at all, and yet the tree stands. Blake put it better than I can:

> To see a World in a Grain of Sand  
> And a Heaven in a Wild Flower,  
> Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand  
> And Eternity in an hour.

## Tat Tvam Asi

Uddalaka does not stop at the tree, and this is where it stops being a lesson in botany. He goes straight on to the line for which the Chandogya is most famous:

> स य एषोऽणिमैतदात्म्यमिदं सर्वं तत्सत्यं स आत्मा तत्त्वमसि श्वेतकेतो इति ॥
>
> *sa ya eṣo'ṇimaitadātmyam idaṃ sarvaṃ tat satyaṃ sa ātmā tat tvam asi śvetaketo iti*

"That which is this subtle essence - the whole of this world has That as its Self. That is the Truth. That is the Self. That thou art, Swetaketu."

The subtle essence in the seed is not out there somewhere. It is the very same essence that is looking at the seed. Which brings us right back to the intellect. When the intellect breaks the brain down into neurons in search of the thinker, it is doing exactly what Swetaketu did with the seed. It breaks and breaks and, at the end, honestly finds nothing. Uddalaka's advice to his son is not to stop breaking things, but to understand that the thing he is looking for is not going to be found among the pieces. It is the one holding the pieces.

Notice also that Uddalaka's last word before *Tat Tvam Asi* is *śraddhatsva* - have faith. Not blind faith, I believe, but the humility to accept that the intellect has run into the edge of its own territory. For a young man who came home proud of everything he knew, that must have been the hardest lesson of all. I am not sure that it gets much easier with age.
