Some coincidences are too delightful to be dismissed as mere coincidences. A day ago, I was listening to an inspiring lecture by Swami Sarvapriyananda on the Kenopanishad. The very next day, I opened Quora and the first question staring at me was "What is Kenopanishad?" It almost felt like the Upanishad was asking me to pause and ruminate on it a little more. Of course, the cynic in me says that the algorithms of the world know what I have been listening to. But I would rather go with the more charitable explanation 🙃. This post is the result of that rumination. <!--more-->

## A detour through Zen

Before I come to the Kenopanishad, let me take a small detour to China. There is a lovely koan in the Gateless Gate (Wumenguan, Case 19) that I keep going back to. Here it is in my own rendering:

> Zhaozhou asked his teacher Nanquan, "What is the Way?"
>
> Nanquan said, "Ordinary mind is the Way."
>
> Zhaozhou asked, "Should I strive towards it?"
>
> Nanquan said, "The moment you strive towards it, you have moved away from it."
>
> Zhaozhou asked, "But if I don't strive, how will I ever know that it is the Way?"
>
> Nanquan said, "The Way is neither a matter of knowing nor of not knowing. To know is to be deluded. To not know is to be blank. When you truly arrive at the Way beyond all doubt, it is as open and vast as the sky. How can one argue about right and wrong here?"
>
> At these words, Zhaozhou was awakened.

(There is a nice commentary on this koan [here](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/monkeymind/?p=23566) if you would like to explore it further.)

Knowing is delusion and not knowing is blankness. Nanquan shuts both doors on his student, and somehow that is exactly what opens the door for him. What amazes me is that a Chinese master in the 9th century and the rishis of the Kenopanishad, separated by centuries and thousands of miles, arrive at almost the same place.

## Mantra 3 - my favourite

The Kenopanishad opens with a deceptively simple question - *keneṣitam patati preṣitam manaḥ* - "Willed by whom does the mind go forth?" And then, a couple of verses later, comes the mantra that I love the most:

> न तत्र चक्षुर्गच्छति न वाग्गच्छति नो मनः ।
> न विद्मो न विजानीमो यथैतदनुशिष्यात् ॥
> अन्यदेव तद्विदितादथो अविदितादधि ।
> इति शुश्रुम पूर्वेषां ये नस्तद्व्याचचक्षिरे ॥
>
> *na tatra cakṣur gacchati na vāg gacchati no manaḥ*
> *na vidmo na vijānīmo yathaitad anuśiṣyāt*
> *anyad eva tad viditād atho aviditād adhi*
> *iti śuśruma pūrveṣāṃ ye nas tad vyācacakṣire*

"The eye does not reach there, nor speech, nor the mind. We do not know, nor do we understand, how one could teach it. It is other than the known, and it is beyond the unknown as well. So we have heard from the ancients who explained it to us."

## The tyranny of our so-called intelligence

I have been fascinated for a long time about how we need to suspend what we call our intelligence before we approach this question. Most of us carry a tacit assumption that everything in this world is discernible by the intellect. If something is real, we feel, we should be able to analyse it, measure it, model it and eventually understand it. Those of us who have spent our lives in science or engineering carry this assumption even more strongly. (I plead guilty.)

The Kenopanishad quietly turns this around. The intellect is not the ultimate discerner. It is a by-product of the ultimate discerner. The Upanishad makes this explicit a couple of verses later - *yan manasā na manute yenāhur mano matam* - that which the mind cannot think, but by which the mind itself is thought. Asking the intellect to grasp its own source is a bit like asking the eye to see itself, or asking a torch to shine its light on the battery that powers it. The tool of discernment cannot discern that which makes the discernment possible in the first place.

## But don't throw it away either

If the mantra had stopped at "the eye does not reach there, nor speech, nor the mind", it would have been easy to swing to the other extreme. "Fine, so it is unknowable. Either it does not exist, or we can just set it aside and get on with our lives." This is the agnostic's comfortable full stop and, I must confess, it is a tempting one.

The rishis close that door too. It is other than the known (*viditāt*), yes. But it is also beyond the unknown (*aviditāt adhi*). It is not just another item on our list of things we don't know yet, waiting for the next research paper. Declaring it "unknowable" and moving on is just another conclusion of the same intellect - only a lazier one. Isn't it?

This is exactly Nanquan's point. "Knowing is delusion" is the first trap, where we believe that the intellect has captured it. "Not knowing is blankness" is the second trap, where we use our ignorance as a convenient excuse to stop.

## Questioning the knower

So where does that leave us? I believe the Kenopanishad is not asking us to stop wanting to know. The wanting is the whole point - after all, the Upanishad itself begins with a question. What it asks us to do is to examine the instrument that we use for knowing. We mistake the intellect for the knower, when it is really a tool in the hands of the knower. Once we see this, the question shifts from "What is Brahman?" to "Who is it that is asking?" And that, I think, is where the real inquiry begins. The Upanishad goes on to say it beautifully in the very next section - it is known to the one who does not claim to know it, and the one who claims to know it does not know it.

Zhaozhou awakened when he heard Nanquan's words. I cannot claim anything so dramatic. But this mantra has done more to humble my own "intelligence" than almost anything else I have read. And I am grateful to the coincidence of a lecture and a Quora question for making me sit down and write about it.
